There are a number of tournament series available in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu and submission grappling today, and many of those have different rulesets. Each ruleset usually purports to somehow promote "purer” Jiu-Jitsu, or at least subjectively better experiences for athletes and spectators.  However, athletes and spectators alike still frequently complain that these rules are inadequate, sub-optimal, or just plain “boring”.  In this piece, I will examine a number of popular scoring systems, where I believe their shortcomings lie, and explain why I believe that a simplified, position-oriented scoring system could result in a better, more honest competitive experience for all parties.

**IBJJF:** By far the most common points system used in Jiu-Jitsu today, with most a majority of smaller regional competitions either using their rules outright or at least using them as a basis for their own.  This is most likely for two reasons: the IBJJF World Championship is beyond a doubt the most prestigious title an athlete can win in gi Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, and their rules are extremely well detailed, thought-out, and disseminated in the Jiu-Jitsu culture.  I will note here that this piece is purely a critique of rule-sets, not organizations.  There are numerous (some of which are valid, in my opinion) critiques of the IBJJF to be had that have nothing to do with their rule set, but that is a topic for another day.

There are a lot of strengths to the IBJJF scoring system; originally created to reward positions and actions that would be favorable in a self-defense, it is the basis for most modern competition rules for a reason.  Besides being undoubtedly the largest and most prestigious tournament organizer in the community, the IBJJF’s system does reward the positions people most commonly identify as dominant in Jiu-Jitsu, and is quite meticulous in its documentation/consideration of various situations.   Unlike with many smaller organizations, a well-versed enough rules expert will almost always be able to explain a given ruling conclusively (or at lest what the ruling should have been).  Likewise, their stringent, objective policies on attire, while criticized by some as draconian, should in theory leave nothing to subjectivity.   Moreover, their well-articulated rules possess a safety-consciousness that many organizations lack, both proactively and reactively banning techniques proven to cause unreasonably high injury rates (kani-basami, spiking, hikkikomi/sumi gaeshi reversal vs the head outside single, etc.)   Indeed, I believe that the IBJJF ruleset often receives far more vitriolic criticism than it deserves, perhaps more due to resentment over the size and dominance of the organization than the rules themselves.

One major criticism levied at IBJJF rules is that they incentivize guard-pulling.  This is largely argued based on the premise that sweeping is easier than passing at the top level, and so people will pull guard to have easier access to a scoring position.   Based on the number of sweeps seen in black belt matches vs passes, and the importance of scoring first (source), there is more than likely some truth to this.  Indeed, at lower weight classes the double guard pull became so prevalent a few years ago that a special rule had to be put in place to prevent it from becoming the dominant strategy.

Perhaps the oldest criticism of the IBJJF rules though lies in the advantage system.  In IBJJF tournaments, players can be awarded “advantages”, or special minor points only considered in the case of a tied score after the match has run its course with no submission.  These advantages are usually earned by *nearly* performing a scoring action, although there are some more objective positions in which advantages are awarded (forcing the bottom player to turtle to avoid a guard pass, fully flattening the half guard.)  The subjectivity issues in this system should be obvious, and when it is coupled with reluctance to disqualify top competitors for stalling, it can create farcical scenes where 2 extremely skilled athletes attempt to win the world championship by pretending to foot lock each other theatrically for several minutes.

The final major criticism (that we will discuss) that is often directed at the IBJJF ruleset is that it does not adequately discourage stalling.  When neither player is making major attempts to progress the game, the IBJJF rules indicate that the ref should give both players a penalty.  Unless one player already has more penalties than the other and is near disqualification, this does exactly nothing.  Because stalling calls are usually handed out fairly slowly, and three stalling calls are needed before an athlete will be disqualified, matches where both competitors have the same number of penalties often devolve into neither competitor being willing to commit vs their opponent’s defensive game-plan.  While a double DQ for extreme stalling (when both competitors receive 3 penalties) does happen on some rare occasions, it is so uncommon, and the stalling when it does happen is so flagrant, that it cannot be said to do anything close to an adequate job of solving the stalling issue.

**EBI**: While nowhere near as common as they once were, EBI rules really only have one flaw, but it is a massive one: overtime.

The basic EBI format features a ten-minute submission-only period wherein all major submissions are legal, and the athletes are incentivized to genuinely attempt to submit with the promise of a cash prize. While the cash prize could not be universally applied to all competitions, and a paring down of the legal submission list may be necessary for safety concerns at different skill levels, those are small alterations any grappling organization could apply easily and organically.  I do not believe there is any real issue in EBI’s regulation period that could not be modified if needed.

The overtime, however, remains a point of massive contention, and for good reason.  A summary of EBI overtime rules is as follows:

- Competitors flip a coin to decide who goes first
- Competitors choose to start attacking from the back or 'spiderweb' position (armbar)
- Competitors take turns to attack from chosen position
- If one competitor submits, and the other does not (opponent escapes), he is declared winner
- If both competitors escape, we move to the next overtime round (maximum 3 overtime rounds)
- If there are no submissions at the end of the three overtime rounds, the competitor with the fastest combined escape time wins the match.

The issue with this should be obvious: by simply surviving the initial 10-minute period, competitors are automatically granted access to a choice of two of the strongest attacking positions in grappling, sometimes versus a grappler against whom they would have virtually no chance of acquiring those positions on in normal circumstances.  There is a reason that certain big-name grapplers never competed in EBI even when it was a highly lauded feature event: some of these grapplers have names and reputations worth far more than the 5,000$ per match EBI awards for submissions in regulation.  Even more, other grapplers without such names are also aware of this.  Rafael Mendes was probably the most conspicuous absence from the EBI events during their heyday, and it makes perfect sense why he was not there.  He was already a huge name at the time, had everything to lose, and almost nothing to gain.  Furthermore, the competition specifically disincentivized skills he had spent his entire life building, namely his guard retention and early escapes.  No one at any of the early EBIs could have hoped to secure full back mount vs Rafael in regulation, but anyone who could shell up for 10 minutes against him could have had it and a chance to claim they tapped the featherweight GOAT for the rest of time.  I am hardly alone in voicing this criticism but I believe this example highlights what I believe to be the flaws in EBI overtime rules.

**True Submission Only:**  Undoubtedly, a true submission only contest with no time limit is the purest test of grappling skill.  Unfortunately, it is completely unfeasible to hold major competitions in this format. Simply put, true submission only matches have wildly varying lengths and action levels, making them extremely unfriendly towards tournament organizers trying to run a timely event, and potentially just as unfriendly towards spectators watching for enjoyment.  Those who have watched a long submission only match can attest that almost all entertaining dynamism is *gone* by the 20 minute mark, and it is not all that unusual for true sub only matches to go near or beyond an hour in length.  While I believe true sub-only should stick around as a sort of ultimate proving ground for super fight matchups, for the reasons I have outlined it will never be a viable option for hosting even decent-sized regional events, let alone a 4000+ competitor world championship.

**Sub Only w/Time Limit and/or Judges:**

This is a section I almost feel like I do not need to write.  The problem with this format is extremely obvious: subjectivity and a lack of resolution.  While it can work in super-fight only cards like UFC BJJ and Polaris events where both competitors get paid regardless, tournament bracketing necessitates that the majority of matches have a decisive winner, and leaving every match to a highly subjective judge’s decision would almost certainly be disastrous.  Particularly at the local and regional level, where there is less scrutiny, the potential for abuse and/or incompetence with judging would be staggering.  Regardless of what criteria was laid out for the judge to (theoretically) evaluate, questionable decisions would be rampant, and competitions’ integrity would suffer.  People are regularly livid with professional boxing/MMA judges who undergo extreme scrutiny and a great deal of training.  I hesitate to use the phrase “shitstorm” in a piece of serious writing, but I must admit that it springs to mind to describe what would happen if every grappling match at a tournament were to be subjectively judged.


**ADCC:**

While ADCC determinedly markets itself as a “submission wrestling” event and not a no gi Jiu-Jitsu competition, ever division at ADCC has consistently been won by a BJJ athlete for well over a decade.  For that reason, I will treat it here like any other ruleset.  While ADCC features a different point distribution per action/position than IBJJF and others commonly use, it mostly scores the same actions/positions.  The major things that separate ADCC from other rulesets are the divided point/no point periods, the guard pulling penalty, and the differing rules in the final match of each bracket.

I have no real issue with dividing matches into no points and then points-having periods. The thinking behind this is that it encourages competitors to go all out for the submission in the first period because they do not have to worry about giving up points if they are reversed or passed in the attempt.  While a metagame around this rule exists (mostly around pulling guard right before the 5 min mark to avoid the -1 negative point), the way competitors game this part of the system is fairly inoffensive and not a major issue.  While I wouldn’t propose separate no point/point periods in my ideal ruleset, it isn't something I think is worth spending time taking arms against, rhetorical or otherwise.

ADCC, as part of its insistent branding as a “submission wrestling” event, is one of the only major organizations to consistently penalize guard pulling.  While I subscribe to the more BJJ-oriented ethos of guard as a relatively neutral position, and do not support major penalties for the pull, there is nothing strictly wrong with this practice, and the rule is cleanly defined and enforceable.  Competitors, during the points period, and uniformly delivered a -1 penalty for guard pulls lasting >3 seconds.  I would argue that, if anything, the penalty should be -2 if they are going to have it, because a pull and a takedown that does not transition cleanly into a pin have the same end result.

My largest criticism with ADCC rules is in the difference between the finals match rules and the “qualifying rounds” rules.  I am not the first (or hundredth) to voice this criticism, but I must ask…why?  Why make the final round of a competition significantly different in terms of the skills it tests than all other rounds?  It makes no sense.  The final match in ADCC brackets is twice as long (for absolutely no real reason) as previous rounds, and negative points are enabled during the full length of the match.  This makes strategies that are viable all the way up until the finals suddenly very risky in the gold medal match.  It simply makes no sense.  The guard pull->leglock strategy during the no point phase strategy that is one of the most common (and effective) strategies until the point in the competition suddenly becomes high-risk almost to the point of unviable.  Flying submission attempts that are heavily encouraged by the ruleset in the early minutes become fool’s errands. It changes the rules enough that they suddenly prioritize a different set of skills/type of competitor, seemingly for no reason and only for the finals match. It is almost impossible to emphasize how weird and harmful this is, but the rich history of strange and boring ADCC finals matches does a decent job of making a case.

This all brings me to my proposed system, and why I think it would go a long way towards creating a better experience for both competitors and spectators. The system itself actually very simple:

- 1 Point for Side Control 
- 2 Points for Mount
- 2 Points for Back control with two hooks
- No advantages or half points of any kind
- One round, scaled up in time per belt/skill level (patterned after the IBJJF)
- A strict ban on guard jumping and kani basami.
- In the event of a draw, the match is scored holistically (and subjectively) based on who got closer to both submissions and dominant position.

That of course is in no way exhaustive; any comprehensive ruleset should include parameters about acceptable attire, sportsmanlike behavior, stalling calls, etc.  However, I want to focus here on what I believe is the core of a grappling ruleset: what scores, what determines who wins when both players follow the rules, and why.

In the interest of justifying these decisions, I will begin by covering
the point distribution, which is probably the most important bit.  More specifically, I would like to explore *why* so few positions score, why no actions score, and why that is the core of this system. 

I expect the most ardent points of contention over this ruleset to be about my omission of sweep and takedown points.  However, sharper eyes will catch that points are not awarded for the action of passing the guard either.  Simply put, I value the submission above all else in grappling, and believe that a majority of Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu competitors do as well.   It has always made little sense to me that we traditionally score the *positions* of mount and back mount because of their usefulness in submitting (or pummeling) an opponent, but we score the *actions* of takedown/pass/sweep.  While all of those (usually) result in an improvement of position, they are not positions in and of themselves.  The inconsistency here is further highlighted when we consider that takedowns/sweeps into side control are treated no differently than those that land directly in the full guard.  If the full guard is considered a neutral position, why do we reward people for attaining it?  They are arriving at the same point as if the opponent simply pulled guard on them, and that is almost always an unscored action.  Some may argue here that the answer is penalizing the guard, but I would submit that they are both misguided and missing the point.  To penalize the guard pull, one of two value judgments must be made: either guard bottom is an inferior position, or takedowns deserve to be artificially incentivized despite guard *not* being an inferior position.
