*Or: How To Not Get Beat Up By Fat People*

If you watch enough competitive Jiu-Jitsu, you can almost certainly pick out styles that are generally preferred by a certain body types. These archetypes include the lanky spider guard player, who prefers to pull guard and leverage his tremendous length and flexibility to tie opponents in knots and stretch them off their base.  Then there is the short fireplug of a grappler, who utilizes his/her low center of gravity to shoot in and take opponents down, beginning the match with a lead so that they may ~~stall in half guard~~ attack from a position of strength and safety. These are just a couple of examples, but the point is that there are a number of styles in Jiu-Jitsu that do an excellent job of maximizing relative attributes for a competitive advantage.  For the career competitor, developing such a style makes a good deal of sense.  It is their goal to maximize effectiveness within their weight class, where opponents will reliably be close to their own weight and possess (generally) similar levels of physical strength.  Moreover, prolific competitors often have intense enough training schedules that they develop the skill level necessary to defeat grapplers far outside their own weight class through sheer superiority, regardless of what their game is optimized for.  However, for those who are not professional competitors (and maybe even for competitors if they are interested in the absolute division), it may be worthwhile to steer away from this sort of game, and instead lean into techniques that adhere to what I call the "universality" principle.  This principle states that the best techniques are the ones that work for anyone, on anyone, regardless of size, strength, or flexibility.

It is no secret to anyone who knows me or anyone familiar with this idea that I have taken a great deal of inspiration here from Marcelo Garcia.  The universality principle I have outlined heavily parallels his philosophy, and so from his game we can draw several examples of techniques that satisfy this principle.  A prime example of which is an all-grappling-sports classic: the arm drag.  The arm drag is such a beautiful technique because it functions so well regardless of the opponent’s size and strength.  If *uke* is much larger and stronger than *tori*, their resisting pull vs the arm drag can be used to launch *tori’s* body into position for an attack. Conversely, if *tori* is larger and stronger than *uke*, *tori* can end up simply pulling *uke* out of position using their momentum and bodyweight.  While the two processes might appear different *visually,* the end result of the arm drag is similar regardless of relative size, which is what makes it such a good example of a “universal” technique.

If the arm drag can be used as a golden example of a technique that satisfies this principle, what then is an example of a technique that does not satisfy this principle?  I would submit two examples, here, one being the triangle and the other being the Kimura.  Including the triangle here is probably tantamount to blasphemy for some, but I will stand by it.  Simply put, there are a large number of bulky, no-neck having individuals that are unreasonably difficult for shorter, thicker grapplers to triangle.   Even people with normal proportions can be completely stymied in a technically clean triangle attempt by 5’8”, 300lbs monsters.  Some readers may begin formulating a counter-argument here, pointing to video explanations and examples where some champion or guru explains how with the perfect triangle anyone can be strangled.  While it is true that almost anyone can complete the triangle on a compliant giant regardless of their own size, it is simply not realistic to expect it to be done in a live setting.  Orlando Sanchez, for example, will never be triangle by a midweight or below competitor, and I would be willing to bet every dollar to my name on that.  Does that mean I advocate throwing the triangle away entirely?  Of course not.  The ability to punish reckless grapplers for artlessly trying to lift a leg is too valuable to give up.  Rather, I am suggesting that size dependent techniques like the triangle should not be the *primary* focus of most grapplers interested in adhering to the universality principle.   That does not mean the skills associated with catching a triangle need go to waste though, regardless of who the opponent is.   For this I will point to the triangle’s very popular cousin: the omoplata.  While the omoplata has an exotic Portuguese name and is considered fancy by some more curmudgeonly old schoolers, it really is no more complicated than the triangle, and is far more universal.  Even the mightiest of foes must succumb to 2 legs opposing their one arm, particularly if they are on brought to their knees and unable to posture away with the full power of their lower body.  This is relevant because almost every triangle setup also contains an omoplata opportunity, and so any time spent learning to catch triangles is not wasted, and can be applied almost immediately to catching a more universally applicable technique.

To take that idea even further, in some instances it is even possible to use the same technique in a different way to better follow the universality principle.  This is evidenced in the way a number of middle weight and lighter grapplers use the aforementioned kimura.  While the kimura is often first introduced to students a submission from closed guard or side control, it is extremely rare in practice to see lightweight grapplers forcing kimura submissions onto much heavier grapplers.  Make no mistake: while a fully locked in kimura from side control possesses the leverage to allow anyone to destroy The Incredible Hulk’s shoulder, separating The Hulk’s arm from his center and placing it behind his back is another matter entirely. Even with perfect execution, the most powerful grip breaks can struggle to work there against a sufficiently bigger and stronger opponent.  This is why many high-level lighter grapplers, including but not limited to Rafael Mendes, Caio Terra, Vagner Rocha, and many others utilize the kimura very effectively as a tool to pass the guard or attack the back/armlock, but rarely complete it as a submission.


There are, of course, limitations to this principle.  Firstly, no one’s game is going to adhere to it *perfectly*.  Even Marcelo Garcia, who I explicitly based much of this thought on, does a fair number of things that do not pass the universality check.  Case in point: Marcelo often utilizes a 2 on 1 arm control from butterfly guard to cross the opponent’s arm over his centerline, and from there lifts the opponent with 2 hooks and kicks out their base so that they land in his lap for a back take.  This has obvious limits based on his opponent’s relative size (Marcelo only weighs so much, and only has so much inertia to help him lift grounded opponents), and I have never seen him use this as a go-to technique against much larger grapplers. Furthermore, it is illogically stubborn for some people not to use the attributes they are born with.  I have one student who is a lanky 6’7”.  It would be absurd for him to completely forgo triangles because there exists a small handful of behemoths that would be difficult for even him to triangle.  He can comfortably triangle 99.999% of the population, and 100% in his own weight class.  For reasonable purposes, that is close enough to universal.  Likewise, it is not bad for shorter grapplers to favor a more shot-heavy style of takedowns.  They are going to find them generally easier than something like trying to uchi mata everyone with their stubby legs, and it is not as though their skills with a short will suddenly become useless when confronted with an opponent shorter than themselves.   Indeed, I am not trying to stop people from using the Jiu-Jitsu that best suits their body; I am trying to encourage them to *prioritize* the Jiu-Jitsu that works for them on *every* body.  The aforementioned arm drag will serve both the lanky gumby man and the fire hydrant well, and against any opponent, which is why I would recommend favoring such techniques whenever possible.

I would also like to include a small disclaimer that while there are a number of techniques that satisfy this ideal, none of this is to say that things won’t almost always be *harder* against a bigger, stronger opponent.  Indeed, while there are a few techniques that are easier when one is the smaller of two grapplers (almost always leg entanglement/leg riding positions), I do not believe there are *any* techniques that are aided by being weaker than one’s opponent.   The road to defeating much larger, more powerful opponents will be long and difficult no matter the pool of techniques you may choose to walk it with, but by following this universality principle you can at least give yourself a much better fighting chance.
