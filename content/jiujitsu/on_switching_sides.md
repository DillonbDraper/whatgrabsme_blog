*When To Let The Hate Flow Through You In BJJ*

*Thesis: In this paper, I shall attempt to explain why the ability to “switch sides” in Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu is actually a relatively “high level” ability, and that it is often somewhat poorly understood by practitioners in a specific way.*

Often, in discussions of training methodologies for Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu, the topic of “training both sides” or “practicing on your dark side” will be broached.  Inevitably, the question that is asked is whether or not one should train moves to both sides, in the sense of drilling one’s techniques ambidextrously rather than focusing on a favored side.  Currently, the “enlightened” answer that is commonly offered is “no, but you need to have an answer to both sides.”  While I largely agree with this view, I would also submit that it is inherently somewhat reductive, and that is offered more due to the necessary time constraints that exist in the gym than because it fully answers the question.  Instead, I argue that it one can fairly easily separate techniques into three categories: techniques that must be learned to both sides, techniques that can be beneficial to learn to both sides, and techniques that really only have to be learned to one side.

What techniques, then, MUST be learned to both sides?  The answer (largely) is defensive technique and escapes.  Consider the following scenario: one spends their entire white belt phase, and much of their blue belt phase playing guard almost exclusively against passers that prefer to pass to the passers left/guard player’s right (90+% of Jiu-Jitieros).  Later, shortly after being awarded a purple belt, the student encounters, in either the gym or competition, encounters a good top player who passes very well to the passer’s right. It is very likely that the guard playing purple belt will be passed very quickly and easily, potentially resulting in an embarrassing defeat in which they offer little resistance.  I would wager that an experience similar to this has been experienced by many of the readers of this piece, and admit freely that it has happened to me more than once in various positions.  In light of this, I would argue that guard retention is a skill that must be drilled and practiced to be both sides, as it is almost always the passer who chooses which side the bottom player must defend Similarly, when escaping submissions or inferior positions, the defender rarely gets to choose the limb they are protecting, or the side to which they must attempt an escape.  Furthermore, while one might prefer different escapes for different sides, it is once again often dependent on the attacker’s positioning in terms of which escapes will be available, indicating a strong need to understand *at least* the basic maneuvers reasonably well to both sides. This brings us to an extremely important point that will be reiterated throughout this argument: that in grappling, the attacker will ultimately dictate the skillset the defender utilizes, which should emphasize to us the importance of being “first” in any grappling engagement.

Next, what of the techniques that fall into the second category, or moves that can be trained to both sides with beneficial results.  In my opinion, this is a category largely comprised of offensive techniques from neutral or semi-neutral positions. Namely, takedowns, sweeps, and guard passes. One of the primary reasons for this is obvious: most people are significantly better at defending one side, and so a dual-sided attack offers a greater chance of winning the crucial neutral game. Beyond that, however, because most people tend to lead with a particular side, especially when standing or passing, it is necessary to have an answer to both left and right handed approaches.  While this answer does not have to be the same on both sides (and in some peoples’ cases, often involves forcing the favored side

One could also further divide this category into techniques that are either “foundational” or “incidental” to one’s game, which will be different for everyone.  For an example of what I mean by this, consider the popular (but polarizing) Miyao brothers; whatever one’s opinion of their game might be, it is inarguable that their innovative and skillful application of the berimbolo and kiss of the dragon sweeps/back takes has been the major part of their success.  It would also be very hard to dispute the idea that their game has significantly influenced competitive Jiu-Jitsu at the lighter weight classes.  Therefore, I would argue that these techniques, while they would never be considered “fundamentals” in the broader sense, should be considered *foundational* to the Miyaos’ specific games.

The last category of moves is those that I believe will/should often be learned primarily to one side, namely offensive attacks.  Probably the most illustrative position for this is the mount.  Simply put, if you have been doing Jiu-Jitsu for any length of time, you are probably aware that it is fairly easy to shut down even a savvy opponent’s escape attempts to one side.  Taking this into consideration, it seems obvious that the top player can more or less choose the exposed side to attack when in the mount.  Therefore, it would stand to reason that it would be a more efficient use of time to drill mostly towards ones favored or natural side when practicing mount attacks; situations where one might have to begin a collar choke or force the elbow with one’s dark side will simply be too rare to matter.

Lastly, and I only include this for the sake of less experienced readers, I must emphasize that virtually no one will ever be truly ambidextrous in their approach to Jiu-Jitsu. Even if one drilled both sides totally evenly, and made a conscious effort to use to both sides while rolling, natural proclivities and opponent tendencies would invariably cause you to favor one side.  I close here by emphasizing that that is okay, even for the techniques I have argued should be practiced ambidextrously; I am sure that even Rickson Gracie has a preferred side on which he demonstrates his escape skills, and it is more than alright that you do too.  Just take care that you avoid the previously outlined pitfalls.
